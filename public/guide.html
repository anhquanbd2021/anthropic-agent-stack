Routing guide

The one rule

Send each task to the cheapest model that can actually do it — then verify. The ladder exists so you can stop paying judgment-tier prices for volume-tier work, and stop shipping volume-tier answers to judgment-tier problems.

How the lab models it

  • Difficulty — every task has a true difficulty (what it actually needs) and an estimated difficulty (what your cheap classifier guessed). Estimates are wrong sometimes — that is the point.
  • Capability — every tier has a capability ceiling. A tier produces a passing answer only when capability ≥ trueDifficulty.
  • Verification — a self-check runs after every attempt and costs a fraction of a volume attempt (VERIFY_RATE = 0.2 units per unit of work). If it fails, the router escalates one tier and retries.
  • Cost — each attempt bills costPerUnit × units; each verification bills the verify rate. The ledger keeps the books.

The three policies

  • Routed + verified — cheapest sufficient tier from the estimate, verify, escalate on fail. Usually the cheapest way to get every task right.
  • Always flagship — judgment tier for everything, verified. Same correctness, at ladder-top prices.
  • Cheapest, unverified — volume tier, no self-check. Lowest bill on paper; watch the accepted-wrong column.

What to look for

  • On mixed-workload: t09 and t10 are estimated easy and aren't — watch them climb the ladder one tier per failed self-check.
  • On misjudged-workload: the estimator is wrong about everything. Routed+verified still lands every task — but the retry burn makes it cost more than always-flagship. Verification is the floor; the estimator is where the savings live.
  • The ledger row accepted-wrong is the failure verification exists to catch.

Honest limits

  • Capabilities, difficulties, and costs are illustrative units — not real model prices or benchmark scores.
  • "Verification" is a deterministic capability check, not a real LLM eval. Real verifiers have their own error rates and their own cost curve.
  • The misjudged workload seeds estimation error by hand; a real router also has to learn when its estimates drift.
  • Escalating one tier at a time is a policy choice — jumping straight to the top after a failure is also defensible when latency matters.
